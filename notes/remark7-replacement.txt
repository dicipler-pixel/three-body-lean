REMARK 7 (replace the whole remark)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Remark 7 (Why the closing is quadratic, and what the symmetry does not do). The equilateral
configuration is fixed, up to orientation, by the relabelling group: the stabiliser of a pole
acts on the tangent plane through an element of order three, whose characteristic polynomial
has discriminant -3 and so no real root. The real 2-plane is therefore irreducible, and every
invariant symmetric 2-tensor at the pole is a multiple of the identity. This is a pointwise
statement about the tensor at the fixed point.

It does not extend to the first order, and it is worth saying so plainly. The symmetric family
Q(x,y) = (1/2)[[2 + x, -y], [-y, 2 - x]] is positive definite on the disc x^2 + y^2 < 4 -- and
not beyond it, where the direction (0,1) gives 1 - r/2 <= 0 -- and invariant under the full D3
stabiliser of the pole, yet its first jet is anisotropic: its eigenvalues are
1 -+ sqrt(x^2 + y^2)/2, so the gap is sqrt(x^2 + y^2) rather than its square. The unoriented
eigenline does come back to itself, but the field of unit eigenvectors does not: on each circle of
radius r > 0 about the pole, a continuous unit eigenvector field for the larger eigenvalue returns
negated after one circuit, and the flip is forced. Without the unit-norm hypothesis it is false,
any nonvanishing scalar multiple of an eigenvector field being again one; and at r = 0 the
eigenvalue is repeated, so "larger" has no meaning. Symmetry alone therefore does not forbid the
conical alternative that Sec. 8 associates with breaking it.

What makes the closing quadratic here is the fold. Phi is the restriction of one linear map, so
the tangent planes at w and at -w coincide and the differentials agree on them; hence
M(-w) = M(w), where in the coordinates of Sec. 2 the antipode is (vartheta, phi) ->
(pi - vartheta, phi + pi). The explicit formula diag(cos^2 vartheta, 1) is even in the normal
coordinate, so its odd Taylor coefficients vanish, and the surviving quadratic term is the
anisotropy computed in Theorem 5, sin^2 vartheta, whose leading term is vartheta^2. The integer
winding is separate again: the soft eigenline is e_vartheta, which is smooth and single-valued
along latitude circles.

The order of the degeneracy is thus a property of the distance map, not of the relabelling
group. The group explains why the two poles are indistinguishable, and nothing further.

SEC. 8, p5 - replace the opening sentence
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
CURRENT: "Theorem 5 attributes the trivial holonomy to Z3 and therefore predicts that breaking
the mass symmetry should produce the generic alternative. It does."

REPLACEMENT: "The equal-mass operator is a fold at each pole, so its gap closes quadratically.
Breaking the mass symmetry destroys that normal form, and Proposition 13 shows the generic
conical behaviour replacing it."

(Reason for the change: Theorem 5's statement says nothing about Z3; the attribution is in
Remark 7, and as the counterexample above shows it is not the group that does the work. The
replacement also makes Prop. 13 a test of a normal-form prediction, which is the stronger claim
and the one the numbers actually support.)

WHAT THIS TEXT DOES NOT CLAIM
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
- It does not claim the Z3 action forces the quadratic closing. It says the opposite, with the
  counterexample.
- It does not claim the two poles are related by a symmetry in the sense of forcing the same
  spectrum; the evenness of M is what gives M(w) = M(-w), and the relabelling story is about
  the labelled triangle, not about M.
- It does not claim the conical behaviour at unequal masses is proved; Prop. 13 remains
  numerical, and says so.
