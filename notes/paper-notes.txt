0. WHY THIS FILE EXISTS
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The paper has two layers that must not be confused:
  (i)  a computation: M = dPhi^T dPhi = diag(cos^2 vartheta, 1), gap = sin^2 vartheta,
       gap/vartheta^2 -> 1. These are exact and now formalised.
  (ii) an explanation: WHY the closing is quadratic rather than conical.
       That is Remark 7, and it is a symmetry argument, not a computation.
A formal statement is evidence only for the statement it states. So each explanation
in the paper needs its own line-by-line check, and the check has to be able to come
out negative. Remark 7 is the first one we ran.

1. WHAT IS CLAIMED, IN THREE PLACES (the chain paper -> README -> Lean)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Paper, Remark 7 (v3, p.3), verbatim:
  "The equilateral configuration is fixed by the Z3 relabelling of the three bodies,
   and an invariant symmetric 2-tensor at a fixed point of that action must be a
   multiple of the identity to leading order. The linear anisotropy is forbidden by
   symmetry, which is why the degeneracy is quadratic and the winding integer rather
   than half-integer. A generic three-body problem with unequal masses breaks Z3 and
   is the place to look for a genuine conical intersection."

README table row: "A symmetric 2-tensor commuting with the 120 deg rotation is a
multiple of the identity" -> z3_isotropic.

Lean, ThreeBody/Basic.lean:
  noncomputable def rot : Matrix (Fin 2) (Fin 2) R := !![-1/2, -(sqrt3/2); sqrt3/2, -1/2]
  theorem z3_isotropic (a b d : R) (h : rot * !! [a, b; b, d] = !! [a, b; b, d] * rot) :
      b = 0 AND a = d

Three different statements are being used as one. The translation:
  paper "invariant"  -> repo "commuting"      (equivalent here only because rot is
                                               orthogonal: rot^T rot = 1 is not in Lean)
  paper "at a fixed point of that action" -> Lean: a bare 2x2 matrix statement, no
                                               action, no fixed point, no tangent space
  paper "to leading order" -> Lean: exact zeroth order, and nothing about the next order
LIMITATIONS.md already says this much ("a statement about 2x2 matrices, not about the
Z3 action on shape space"), but the README row reads as if it were the paper's claim.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2. WHAT THE LEAN THEOREM ACTUALLY GIVES  [x]
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
True, and its proof is complete: rot here is rotation by +120 deg (det 1, order 3);
a symmetric M commuting with it is scalar. This is the zeroth-order statement: at the
fixed point, an invariant symmetric 2-tensor on the 2-plane is a multiple of I.
Sharpness: the hypothesis needs sin(theta) != 0. For rot = -I (180 deg) every symmetric
M commutes and the conclusion fails; for a generic angle != 0, pi it holds, because the
commutant of a rotation with sin != 0 is {alpha I + beta J} and symmetry forces beta = 0.
So what does the work is IRREDUCIBILITY of the real 2-dim representation (no invariant
line), not the order 3. Order 3 is not what is being used, even though the theorem is
named for it. Worth one sentence in the paper; a name that overstates the content is a
claim too.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3. THE FINDING: "THE LINEAR ANISOTROPY IS FORBIDDEN BY SYMMETRY" IS FALSE  [x]
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Test: let L: R^2 -> Sym^2(R^2) be a first-order (linear) term of a symmetric tensor
family at the fixed point, and ask for equivariance L(g.p) = g L(p) g^T.

  Z3 only (g = rot, order 3):        solution space is 2-DIMENSIONAL. Nonzero linear
                                     terms exist. (sympy, .scratch/audit/linear_term2.py)
  Z3 plus one reflection (full D3
  stabiliser of the pole):           still 1-DIMENSIONAL. A nonzero linear term
                                     survives the whole relabelling group.

So equivariance does not forbid the linear term; the relabelling symmetry is not the
reason the closing is quadratic. (Sanity check on the shape of the answer: a traceless
symmetric form is the 2-dim irrep with character e^{2i theta}; a linear map
L(p) = A p must satisfy A R = R^{-2theta} A, i.e. A R = R A for theta = 120 deg,
which is satisfied by A complex-linear, a 2-parameter family.)

What does forbid it, in this problem: Phi(w) = (w1, w2) is the restriction of a LINEAR
map to T_w S^2, and T_{-w} = T_w, so dPhi_{-w} = dPhi_w as maps of that same plane.
Hence M(-w) = M(w) exactly: M is EVEN in the normal coordinate at the pole, so every
odd-order term vanishes, and the linear one first of all. Equivalently nabla M = 0 at
the pole, by the point symmetry w -> -w of the sphere there. This is a fact about this
Phi, not about Z3. [x] M(w) = M(-w): diag(cos^2 vartheta, 1) with (vartheta, phi) ->
(pi - vartheta, phi + pi).
Note what is NOT excluded by evenness: the quadratic term is genuinely anisotropic --
M = I - (x^2+y^2) diag(1,0) + O(4) in Cartesian coordinates -- which is exactly
gap = sin^2 vartheta. So the correct statement is: evenness kills the odd orders, the
quadratic term survives and is the gap.

Consequence for the paper's logic: Theorem 5 does not need Remark 7. Theorem 5's proof
computes gap = sin^2 vartheta directly. Remark 7 is an explanation of that computation,
and as written it gives the wrong reason. Two possible repairs:
  (A) keep Remark 7 but attribute the vanishing to evenness (Phi linear, T_{-w} = T_w),
      and keep Z3 only for the zeroth-order statement. One extra sentence.
  (B) demote it: "the closing is quadratic because Phi is the restriction of a linear
      map, so M is even at the pole; the Z3 statement is the pointwise shadow of this."
Either way the "which is why ... the winding integer rather than half-integer" part
needs its own argument (the eigenline is e_vartheta, smooth and single-valued along
latitude circles: that is in Theorem 5's verification, not in Remark 7).

4. HOW THIS SHOULD READ IN THE PAPER (verbal lay-out, first pass)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Keep the order: operator -> two loci -> equilateral -> the remark. But split the
remark into its two orders, because they have different proofs and different status:

  Remark (why no linear term). Three statements, in increasing strength:
   (a) POINTWISE. At the equilateral point the tangent action of the relabelling group
       is a rotation, and the real 2-plane carries an irreducible representation of it;
       hence every invariant symmetric 2-tensor there is a multiple of the identity.
       (Formal: z3_isotropic, the 2x2 core; the passage from "invariant tensor" to
       "commuting matrix" is rot^T rot = 1, not formalised.)
   (b) FIRST ORDER. Equivariance alone does NOT kill the linear term -- here is the
       2-parameter family that survives, and the 1-parameter family that survives the
       full D3. So (a) does not imply (b); a reader who thinks symmetry gives quadratic
       closing for free is wrong, and the paper currently tells them it is free.
   (c) THE ACTUAL REASON. M = dPhi^T dPhi restricted to T_w S^2, Phi linear, so
       dPhi depends only on the unoriented plane: M(w) = M(-w). Even in the normal
       coordinate => no odd terms => quadratic closing, with the quadratic term
       computed to be sin^2.
Also say what the winding integer needs: the soft eigenline is e_vartheta.

In the README table, the z3_isotropic row should say "at the equilateral point the
invariant tensor is scalar (zeroth order)" and not be offered as the reason for the
quadratic closing.

8. ADDENDUM: THE SHARPER TRUE STATEMENT (verified myself, .scratch/audit/verify_members.py)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
(a) THEOREM (what the order-3 hypothesis really gives). Let A be a real 2x2 matrix with
    A^3 = I and A != I. Then every symmetric S with AS = SA is a multiple of I.
    Proof, one line: x^3 - 1 has distinct roots, so A is diagonalisable; A != I forces the
    eigenvalue pair {omega, omega^2}, so A is cyclic and its commutant is span{I, A}.
    If S = alpha I + beta A is symmetric then beta (A - A^T) = 0, and A = A^T would make A
    real-diagonalisable with eigenvalues in {1}, i.e. A = I. Hence beta = 0.
    Consequences: no rotation needed, no angle needed, orthogonality only enters to convert
    "invariant" (R^T M R = M) into "commuting" (MR = RM). That conversion is exactly
    `invariant_iff_commute` in .scratch/audit/Gap.lean.
    Verbal form for the paper: "the relabelling acts through an order-three element, and a
    real 2-plane is an irreducible representation of an order-three element; hence the only
    invariant symmetric 2-tensor at the fixed point is a multiple of the identity." Keep
    "irreducible", drop "120 degrees" as the reason -- 120 is how it happens here, not why.
    [x] Checked in sympy for general theta: R_theta S - S R_theta = [[-2 b sin t, (a-d) sin t],
    [(a-d) sin t, 2 b sin t]], so scalar iff sin t != 0; at t = 0, pi, diag(1,0) commutes.

(b) THE COUNTEREXAMPLE, in the sharpest form (m4's, re-verified): on the tangent plane put
    A(x,y) = (1/2) [[x, -y], [-y, -x]]. Then
      - A is symmetric for every (x,y);
      - A(R(x,y)) = R A(x,y) R^T for R the 120-degree rotation, exactly;
      - char poly = (-4 lambda^2 + x^2 + y^2)/4, so the eigenvalues are +- sqrt(x^2+y^2)/2
        and the gap is sqrt(x^2+y^2): LINEAR, i.e. a genuine conical point, with the
        standard -1 eigenline monodromy.
    So a Z3-symmetric symmetric-tensor family can have a conical point. The paper's
    "the linear anisotropy is forbidden by symmetry, which is why the degeneracy is
    quadratic and the winding integer rather than half-integer" is false as a symmetry
    statement. (Not a curiosity: in two dimensions a C3 axis protects, rather than
    forbids, a Dirac/conical point -- graphene's K points are the standard example. The
    paper's own Sec. 8 finding, that breaking Z3 gives a conical intersection, is
    therefore the right observation with the wrong explanation: what the equal-mass
    problem has that (1,2,3) does not is the explicit form M = diag(cos^2, 1), not merely
    more symmetry.)
    NOTE for the paper: this cuts both ways and is worth saying explicitly. If the
    protection is not group-theoretic, then the sentence "a generic three-body problem
    with unequal masses breaks Z3 and is the place to look for a genuine conical
    intersection" is a good empirical prediction with a wrong justification.

(c) WHERE THE QUADRATIC CLOSING COMES FROM. Two equivalent statements, both checkable:
      - M is even at the pole: M(w) = M(-w), because T_{-w} = T_w and dPhi_{-w} = dPhi_w
        (Phi is the restriction of one linear map). Odd terms vanish; the quadratic term
        survives and is anisotropic.
      - Equivalently: M is a function of the unoriented plane, i.e. of w (x) w / |w|^2.
    In Cartesian coordinates at the pole: M = I - (x^2 + y^2) diag(1,0) + O(4), so the gap
    is exactly x^2 + y^2 to leading order, which is Theorem 5's sin^2 vartheta. Nothing in
    this step uses the relabelling group.

(d) STATUS OF THE ZERO-COST CLAIM. z3_isotropic is TRUE and its proof is complete; the
    README row, the docstring ("no linear anisotropy survives at the equilateral point")
    and the paper's Remark 7 each pass one step beyond it. The theorem covers the tensor
    AT the point; the prose claims the LINEAR TERM is absent. Between them sits a step
    that is false in general and true here for a different reason.

9. THE SHAPE OF THE PAPER SECTION, NOW THAT THE CONTENT IS FIXED
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Point to make in one paragraph, in this order -- each sentence is something we can defend:
  1. The equilateral point is a fixed point of the relabelling action; the tangent action is
     an order-three rotation, and a real 2-plane is irreducible for it. Therefore the only
     invariant symmetric 2-tensor there is scalar. (Zeroth order. Formal: z3_isotropic,
     plus orthogonality to match the paper's word "invariant".)
  2. That does NOT give the quadratic closing, and the paper should say so: an explicit
     order-three-equivariant linear term exists (write it), and it produces a conical point.
     Anyone who thinks symmetry pays for the quadratic term is wrong here.
  3. What pays for it is the fold: Phi is linear, so M depends on the shape only through the
     unoriented tangent plane, hence M(w) = M(-w), hence no odd term, and the quadratic term
     is the computed sin^2 vartheta.
  4. Consequence, stated conservatively: the equilateral degeneracy is quadratic because the
     distance map is a fold there (Prop. 8), not because of Z3. Z3 is why the two equilateral
     points look alike and why the first-order term is not *forced* by anything else -- no,
     better: Z3 plays no role in the closing order at all; it is used only for the
     pointwise isotropy and for the paired structure of the two poles.
  This is a nicer story than the original, because it separates two phenomena the paper
  currently merges, and it makes Prop. 8 (the fold) carry the weight it already carries.

REMARK 7, replacement text:

  Remark 7 (Why the closing is quadratic, and what the symmetry does not do). The equilateral
  configuration is fixed, up to orientation, by the relabelling group: the stabiliser of a pole
  acts on the tangent plane through an element of order three, and that real 2-plane is an
  irreducible representation of it. Hence every invariant symmetric 2-tensor at the pole is a
  multiple of the identity. This is a pointwise statement, and it is the one the formal core
  proves.
  It does not extend to the first order. An explicit symmetric family, Q(v) = 1 + (1/2)[[x, -y],
  [-y, -x]], is positive definite near the origin and invariant under the full D3 stabiliser,
  yet its first jet is anisotropic: its eigenvalues are 1 -+ r/2, its gap is r rather than r^2,
  and its eigenline returns negated after one circuit. Symmetry alone therefore permits exactly
  the conical alternative that Sec. 8 attributes to breaking it.
  What makes the closing quadratic here is the fold: Phi is the restriction of one linear map,
  so the tangent planes at w and -w coincide and dPhi agrees on them, whence M(-w) = M(w). The
  Gram operator is even in the normal coordinate at the pole, every odd term vanishes, and the
  surviving quadratic term is the anisotropy computed in Theorem 5, sin^2 vartheta. The integer
  winding is separate again: the soft eigenline is e_vartheta, which is smooth and single-valued
  along latitude circles.
  The order of degeneracy is thus a property of the distance map, not of the relabelling group.
  The group explains why the two poles are indistinguishable, and nothing else.

What was wrong: I wrote "The eigenline does not come back to itself either". That is false.
Negating a nonzero vector does not change its span, so the unoriented line DOES return:
evec 0 = (1,0) and evec (2π) = (-1,0) are the same line. What fails to return is the field of
unit vectors, not the line. Monodromy -1 is a statement about the framed (oriented) field; the
line field itself is single-valued and has index +1. The sentence was doing double duty for a
correct theorem (evec_monodromy) and an incorrect gloss.

Also from m2, all accepted:
  - "the larger eigenvalue" needs r > 0: at r = 0 the two eigenvalues coincide and "larger" has
    no meaning. The Lean statement has hr : 0 < r for exactly this reason; the prose was silent.
  - M(-w) = M(w) is gram_even only after the coordinate identification of the antipode; the paper
    should state it: in Sec. 2 coordinates (vartheta, phi) -> (pi - vartheta, phi + pi).
  - "every odd term vanishes" should be attributed to the explicit even formula diag(cos^2,1),
    not to gram_even, which is a matrix equality and not a Taylor theorem. sin^2 vartheta is the
    exact gap, whose leading term is vartheta^2 -- not "the quadratic term vanishes".
